\title{Confounder-Robust Flood Detection from Street-Level Imagery via
  Phase-1 Hard Negative Mining}

\author[a]{[AUTHOR ANONYMISED FOR REVIEW]}

\address[a]{[INSTITUTION ANONYMISED FOR REVIEW]}

%%─── Abstract ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
We introduce Phase-1 Hard Negative Mining (\PHNM{}), a confounder-targeted
resampling pipeline for street-level flood detection that exploits a partially
trained intermediate checkpoint to recover visual confounder candidates that a
fully converged model cannot provide.
Existing flood screening systems report aggregate accuracy or ROC-AUC, which
conceal category-level false positive rates and overstate discrimination on
class-imbalanced datasets.
Standard hard negative mining applied to fully converged models produces empty
candidate sets because training convergence eliminates the decision-boundary
uncertainty that mining requires.
We fine-tune \EffNet{} and \ResNet{} with a two-phase progressive transfer
learning protocol on 4,099 deduplicated street-level images spanning eight
non-flood categories including four water confounders, mine difficulty-ranked
candidates from the Phase-1 checkpoint, apply $5\times$ augmentation, and
retrain, ablating against extended-training and random-injection controls.
We evaluate with \PR{} as the primary metric and report per-category false
positive rates with \CP{} 95\,\% binomial confidence intervals, validated with
McNemar pairwise significance tests.
\EffNet{} achieves \PR{}$= 0.9976$ versus \ResNet{}'s $0.9614$---a 3.6-point gap
invisible in ROC-AUC ($0.9985$ vs.\ $0.9728$)---and \PHNM{} improves overall
classification accuracy by 0.49 accuracy percentage points while difficulty-ranked
selection outperforms both extended training and random augmentation alone,
isolating the contribution of Phase-1 difficulty ranking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Floods are the most frequently occurring natural hazard worldwide and among the
most destructive in terms of loss of life and economic damage.
Satellite-based analysis covering 18 years of global data reveals that an
average of 58\,million people were exposed to flooding each year between 2000
and 2018, with the exposed population increasing by 20--24\,\% over that period
\citep{Tellman2021}.
The economic costs of flood events continue to rise as urban development
encroaches on flood plains and as extreme precipitation events become more
frequent under climate change.
Effective early warning and rapid situational assessment are therefore critical
infrastructure priorities.

Automated detection of flooding from crowdsourced street-level imagery offers a
complementary situational awareness channel to traditional gauge networks and
satellite revisit cycles.
When citizens submit photographs via smartphone applications, social media
platforms, or fixed traffic camera feeds, the resulting image stream can
provide hyperlocal evidence of active flooding that precedes official event
reports by hours \citep{Esparza2022}.
The first-pass screening problem is binary: given an image of unknown provenance
and unknown quality, does it depict active flooding?
A screening system that operates at high recall on this binary problem enables
downstream triage, reducing the burden on human analysts who must validate
alerts in real time.

\textbf{The screening objective is recall-first.}
A missed flood image (false negative) means that an affected location is not
escalated for human review; the consequence is delayed response and potentially
preventable harm.
A false positive triggers unnecessary resource allocation but carries a lower
immediate cost.
This asymmetric cost structure makes flood recall---the fraction of actual flood images correctly identified---the primary operational metric.
Performance depends strongly on the evaluation metric chosen, particularly given this recall-focused objective.
Yet most published flood detection systems report ROC-AUC or overall accuracy,
metrics that are known to overstate performance on imbalanced binary
classification problems \citep{Davis2006}: specificity is easy to achieve when
negatives substantially outnumber positives, inflating ROC-AUC even when recall
is poor.
Precision-recall AUC (\PR{}) is the more informative metric for this problem
regime \citep{Davis2006,Saito2015}.
We demonstrate this gap empirically in \cref{sec:results:metric}: a 2.6-point
ROC-AUC difference between \EffNet{} and \ResNet{} corresponds to a 3.6-point
\PR{} difference and a difference of 56 missed floods in an 819-image validation
set.

\textbf{Visual confounders are the dominant failure mode.}
A flood detection classifier trained on street-level images must distinguish
flooded road surfaces from visually similar non-flood scenes.
Swimming pools share an open, flat, reflective water surface, similar colour
statistics under both overcast and sunny illumination, and are embedded in
urban and residential environments indistinguishable in scale from flooding.
Rivers, particularly when photographed at close range from bridges or
embankments, produce images with similar surface texture, ripple patterns,
and reflectivity to flooded streets.
Park water features and ponds introduce the same water texture in urban
green-space settings.
Wet roads after rainfall present specular reflections on dark asphalt that
partially mimic the low-albedo water signal of floods.
Under close-range photography, contextual cues that would disambiguate a pool
from a flood at larger scale are absent: the horizon line, surrounding
architecture, and ground plane are often cropped out or ambiguous.
Standard cross-entropy trained models that exploit water texture as their
primary flood cue will therefore misclassify all of these categories at rates
that depend on the overlap between flood and confounder visual distributions.

Per-category confounder analysis is absent from existing flood detection benchmarks.
Existing benchmarks rarely provide fine-grained confounder category coverage,
and published results report aggregate precision rather than per-category false
positive rates with confidence intervals.
Without per-category statistics, a practitioner cannot determine which visual
scenarios are driving false positives, nor can they target retraining efforts
appropriately.

\textbf{The hard negative mining signal problem.}
Standard offline hard negative mining \citep{Viola2004,Shrivastava2016}
addresses this by identifying the hardest-scoring non-flood images from a
trained model and injecting them into retraining to strengthen the decision
boundary.
However, this approach fails on fully converged two-phase fine-tuned models for
a structural reason: by the end of Phase 2, the model assigns near-zero flood
probability to all training non-flood images regardless of their visual
similarity to floods.
The model has memorised the training distribution; mining from this checkpoint
produces empty candidate lists.
We address this by mining from the Phase-1 intermediate checkpoint, at which
point the model has trained only the classification head and the top backbone
layers.
At this stage, the early ImageNet feature extractors are still frozen, leaving
residual uncertainty on categories that share visual features with the positive
class.
The Phase-1 checkpoint retains genuine confusion on river and pool images that
the Phase-2 checkpoint has resolved---this is the mining signal.
This is a form of \emph{offline} hard negative mining: candidates are identified
in a single pre-training pass and injected into the dataset before retraining,
rather than re-selected within each mini-batch.

\textbf{Research gap.}
State-of-the-art ground-level flood screening systems achieve high aggregate
performance---\citet{Lee2025} report 97.67\,\% recall; \citet{Khan2023} report
96.43\,\% accuracy---yet none measure per-category confounder false positive
rates with confidence intervals.
Existing flood screening work does not evaluate FP rates broken down by
confounder category, does not apply HNM from an intermediate checkpoint,
and does not ablate the difficulty-ranking contribution against extended
training and random data injection.
The absence of ablation controls in prior HNM applications to flood data means
it is unclear whether any observed improvements are attributable to the
difficulty ranking itself or simply to the additional training epochs and
data exposure.
Our experimental design directly addresses this gap.
We also measure per-category FP rates on a held-out validation set with
\CP{} 95\,\% confidence intervals \citep{Clopper1934}, providing the
statistical machinery to assess whether observed improvements are detectable
at the available sample sizes.

\textbf{Contributions.}
This paper makes the following contributions to the flood detection and
remote sensing machine learning literature:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Extended benchmark.}
        We combine the USF FloodingDataset \citep{FloodingDataset} with
        river images from the RIWA dataset \citep{Wagner2023} and apply
        SHA-256 deduplication, producing 4,099 unique street-level images
        across 11 fine-grained categories with a stratified 80/20
        train/val split (seed\,=\,42).
        The benchmark includes fine-grained flood severity labels
        (Major, Moderate, Minor) enabling severity-stratified analysis.

  \item \textbf{PR-AUC as primary metric.}
        We provide an empirical demonstration of a 3.6-point \PR{} gap
        between \EffNet{} ($0.9976$) and \ResNet{} ($0.9614$) that is
        invisible in ROC-AUC ($0.9985$ vs.\ $0.9728$), confirming
        \citet{Davis2006}'s argument for \PR{} on imbalanced
        screening problems and motivating its adoption as the standard
        primary metric for this class of problems.

  \item \textbf{Phase-1 HNM pipeline.}
        A three-stage offline pipeline (\cref{fig:hnm_loop}) that mines
        difficulty-ranked confounder candidates from the Phase-1
        intermediate checkpoint, applies $5\times$ data augmentation, and
        retrains from the converged Phase-2 checkpoint.
        We release the pipeline implementation with the paper.

  \item \textbf{Controlled ablation.}
        We isolate the difficulty-ranking contribution of \PHNM{} by
        ablating against (a) extended training with no additional data
        and (b) random injection of category-matched non-flood images,
        establishing the source of any improvement.

  \item \textbf{Statistical validation.}
        Per-category false positive rates are reported with \CP{}
        95\,\% exact binomial confidence intervals; model comparisons
        use McNemar's pairwise test with Bonferroni correction; bootstrap
        confidence intervals are provided for multi-seed validation
        (pending additional seeds).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Ground-level flood detection}
\label{sec:related:ground}

Ground-level flood detection from crowdsourced imagery has attracted increasing
research attention as the prevalence of smartphone cameras and social media
platforms provides a low-cost source of real-time visual evidence.
\citet{Esparza2022} examined data imbalance in crowdsourced flood reports
submitted via a citizen science platform and demonstrated that class distribution
directly affects model recall, motivating the use of weighted sampling
strategies.
\citet{Papadimos2023} benchmarked a range of multimedia flood detection
approaches---including convolutional networks, graph neural networks, and
multimodal fusion---on a publicly available multimedia flood challenge dataset,
finding that visual features alone achieve competitive performance when training
data is sufficient.
\citet{Khan2023} proposed Flood-ResNet50, a ResNet50 variant optimised for
deployment on resource-constrained edge hardware, reporting 96.43\,\% accuracy
on their evaluation set and demonstrating inference throughput suitable for
real-time operation.
\citet{Lee2025} introduced AlleyFloodNet, a dataset targeting
economically marginalised alley environments---settings particularly vulnerable
to surface flooding but underrepresented in existing benchmarks---and
showed that a ConvNeXt-Large model fine-tuned on this data achieves
97.67\,\% flood recall.
\citet{Alam2023} contributed the MEDIC benchmark for multi-task disaster
image classification, covering floods, wildfires, hurricanes, and other
events with multiple label types.
A common limitation of all these systems is that none report per-category
false positive rates for water-confounding non-flood categories; none
distinguish river from pool from lake from wet-road confusion; and none
report confidence intervals for small-$N$ confounder categories.
Our benchmark and evaluation protocol directly address this gap.

\subsection{Aerial and satellite flood detection}
\label{sec:related:aerial}

A parallel literature addresses flood detection from aerial or satellite
imagery.
\citet{Rahnemoonfar2020} introduced FloodNet, a high-resolution post-Hurricane
Harvey aerial imagery dataset with fine-grained semantic segmentation labels
distinguishing flooded buildings, flooded roads, non-flooded buildings, and
other scene elements.
\citet{Tellman2021} developed a global framework using Landsat and MODIS
satellite time series to estimate the number of people exposed to surface
flooding, providing a macro-scale complement to event-level street-level
detection.
Aerial and satellite approaches benefit from consistent imaging geometry and
large field of view, which resolve many of the visual ambiguities that affect
street-level classification---from altitude, a swimming pool and a flooded
road are trivially distinguishable by context.
Street-level screening therefore addresses a different, complementary problem:
it provides hyperlocal, real-time evidence in the hours following a flood event
before satellite revisit cycles produce updated imagery.
Both approaches are needed in a complete early warning system.

\subsection{Hard negative mining}
\label{sec:related:hnm}

Hard negative mining has a long history in detection pipelines.
\citet{Viola2004} demonstrated that bootstrapped negative mining---adding the
hardest false positive windows from the current detector into the next round of
training---dramatically reduces the false positive rate of the Viola-Jones
face detector with minimal impact on detection rate.
This insight was systematised as online hard example mining (OHEM) by
\citet{Shrivastava2016}: selecting the $K$ highest-loss proposals within each
mini-batch to focus gradient updates on informative examples.
\citet{Lin2017} introduced focal loss as a continuous gradient-level analogue:
down-weighting well-classified examples with a $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ factor,
automatically concentrating learning signal on hard cases without requiring
explicit candidate selection.
Hard negative mining methods differ along a key axis.
\emph{Online} methods (e.g., OHEM~\citep{Shrivastava2016} and focal
loss~\citep{Lin2017}) re-select hard examples within each mini-batch during
an in-progress training run.
\emph{Offline} methods identify candidates in a separate pass and inject them
into the training dataset before the next training phase; they require a model
that still produces informative difficulty scores on the candidate set.
Our approach shares the goal of these methods but differs in three key respects.
First, we use \emph{offline batch} mining: candidates are identified once per
phase and injected into the training dataset, rather than re-selected per
iteration.
Second, we mine from the Phase-1 \emph{intermediate} checkpoint rather than a
fully converged model, because convergence eliminates the mining signal.
Third, we operate at the \emph{data level}---permanently adding new training
examples---rather than at the gradient level.
These differences are not incidental: the data-level injection is precisely what
allows the difficulty-ranking contribution to be isolated from extended training
and random injection in our ablation design.

\subsection{Progressive and catastrophic-forgetting-aware transfer learning}
\label{sec:related:progressive}

Transfer learning from large-scale image classification models (\eg{}
ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 \citep{He2016} or EfficientNet \citep{Tan2019})
is standard practice for small-domain image classification problems.
However, fine-tuning all layers simultaneously on a small target dataset risks
catastrophic forgetting \citep{Kirkpatrick2017}: gradient updates for the new
task overwrite previously learned representations, degrading generalisation.
Progressive fine-tuning strategies mitigate this by gradually unfreezing layers
from the task-specific head toward the backbone.
\citet{Lyu2025} demonstrated that a staged ResNet50 protocol improves
convergence stability and final accuracy on small datasets compared to
full-network fine-tuning or head-only adaptation.
Our two-phase protocol follows this design: Phase\,1 trains the head and top
backbone layers at a moderate learning rate, allowing the network to adapt its
high-level features while preserving low-level texture representations;
Phase\,2 unfreezes deeper layers at a reduced learning rate, allowing gradual
adaptation of mid-level features.
The Phase-1 checkpoint thus captures an intermediate representation state that
is informative for mining but has not yet overfit the training distribution.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics for imbalanced classification}
\label{sec:related:metrics}

The choice of evaluation metric is not a secondary concern for flood
screening but a primary one.
\citet{Davis2006} formally proved that the ROC and precision-recall curves
convey different information and that the PR curve is more informative when
the positive class prevalence is low: ROC-AUC is dominated by the large
number of true negatives (non-flood images), inflating the apparent
discrimination even when the classifier systematically misclassifies the
positive class.
\citet{Saito2015} provided empirical guidance for choosing between ROC-AUC
and PR-AUC, showing through extensive simulation that ROC-AUC misleads
practitioners in precisely the class-imbalanced regimes common in screening
applications.
The critical difference is that PR-AUC penalises false negatives through
the recall axis and false positives through the precision axis, weighting
them in a manner directly relevant to the recall-first screening objective.
For per-category false positive rates on small validation sets, the
normal approximation for binomial confidence intervals fails badly
\citep{Clopper1934}; we use the exact \CP{} interval, which provides
guaranteed frequentist coverage regardless of sample size.
This is especially important for the swimming pool category ($N{=}28$),
where the upper bound of the 95\,\% CI for a zero observed FP rate is 12.3\,\%.

\subsection{Source datasets}
\label{sec:dataset:sources}

The flood images are drawn from the USF FloodingDataset
\citep{FloodingDataset}, a publicly available street-level benchmark
providing binary flood/non-flood labels together with flood severity
annotations at three levels: Major (severe inundation, water above
knee level), Moderate (partial inundation, water on road surface),
and Minor (early flooding, small puddles visible).
These severity classes represent operationally distinct scenarios for which
recall requirements may differ: a Major flood image missed by the screener
has higher operational cost than a Minor one.
The non-flood categories in FloodingDataset include street scenes,
animals, vehicles, buildings, plant life, and parks/walkways.

To supplement the river confounder category---which is the dominant
source of false positives in our baseline models---we incorporate river
images from the RIWA dataset \citep{Wagner2023}.
RIWA is a pixel-wise binary river water segmentation collection of
1,128 labelled images captured with smartphones, drones, and DSLRs
from diverse rivers, originally compiled to benchmark convolutional network
augmentation strategies for river surface segmentation.
We use the image files without segmentation masks, treating all RIWA
images as the `river' non-flood subcategory.
This enriches the river category to 399 images (323 train, 76 val), providing
sufficient sample size to measure river FP rates with reasonable \CP{}
confidence intervals.

\subsection{Deduplication}
\label{sec:dataset:dedup}

Before splitting, we apply SHA-256 hash deduplication to the combined image
pool to remove exact-duplicate files.
This step is necessary because the source datasets may include identical
images distributed under different filenames, and duplicate presence across
train/val boundaries would artificially inflate estimated generalisation
performance.
The deduplication step removed 55 exact-duplicate images, yielding
4,099 unique images.
No perceptual similarity deduplication was applied; near-duplicate
images (\eg{} slightly different crops of the same scene) remain in the
dataset.

\subsection{Stratified split}
\label{sec:dataset:split}

Images are assigned to train (80\,\%) and validation (20\,\%) splits
using stratified sampling by fine-grained category label with random seed\,=\,42.
Stratification ensures that the flood severity distribution (Major/Moderate/Minor)
and the non-flood category distribution are preserved in both splits.
Without stratification, random splits on an imbalanced dataset can produce
train or val sets that over-represent certain categories, biasing both the
learned representations and the evaluation metrics.
The resulting split contains 3,280 training images and 819 validation images.
No test split is held out; threshold selection is performed on the validation
set only, and the paper reports validation set performance throughout
(\cref{sec:methods:eval}).

\subsection{Architectures}
\label{sec:methods:arch}

We evaluate two ImageNet-pretrained convolutional architectures to assess
whether the \PHNM{} pipeline is architecture-agnostic or specific to
particular feature spaces.

\textbf{\EffNet{}} \citep{Tan2019}.
EfficientNet scales network depth, width, and input resolution simultaneously
via a compound scaling coefficient, producing better accuracy per parameter
than architectures scaled on a single dimension.
\EffNet{} has approximately 5.3\,M parameters.
Under Keras 3, the \texttt{include\_preprocessing} argument has been removed;
the built-in rescaling layer inside the model graph handles pixel normalisation,
so the model receives raw $[0, 255]$ pixel values and performs its own
$\text{scale} = \frac{1}{255}$ rescaling internally.
This is a critical implementation detail: passing pre-normalised inputs to
\EffNet{} in Keras 3 produces degraded performance.

\textbf{\ResNet{}} \citep{He2016}.
ResNet50 uses shortcut connections to train networks of 50 residual layers
with approximately 25.6\,M parameters.
Input is preprocessed by channel-mean subtraction using the ImageNet channel
means $(\mu_R, \mu_G, \mu_B) = (103.94, 116.78, 123.68)$ via
\texttt{tf.keras.applications.resnet50.preprocess\_input}.
Mixing the preprocessing conventions between architectures
(\ie{} applying ResNet preprocessing to \EffNet{} or vice versa) is a
documented source of performance degradation in flood-screening code and
was verified at runtime in our implementation.

\textbf{Classification head.}
Both architectures use a shared binary classification head appended after
the backbone:
\begin{equation}
  \text{GAP}(\mathbf{x})
  \xrightarrow{\text{Drop}(0.2)}
  \text{Dense}(256, \text{ReLU})
  \xrightarrow{\text{BN}}
  \xrightarrow{\text{Drop}(0.3)}
  \text{Dense}(1, \sigma)
  \label{eq:head}
\end{equation}
where GAP is global average pooling, BN is batch normalisation, Drop($p$) is
dropout with drop probability $p$, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation.
The output is a scalar $\hat{p} \in [0, 1]$ representing $P(\text{non-flood})$.
The flood probability used for all evaluation is $1 - \hat{p}$.

\subsection{Two-phase progressive fine-tuning}
\label{sec:methods:finetune}

Two-phase progressive fine-tuning mitigates catastrophic forgetting
\citep{Kirkpatrick2017} and has been shown to improve convergence stability
on small datasets compared to full-network fine-tuning \citep{Lyu2025}.
Our two-phase protocol is defined as follows.

\textbf{Phase 1.}
Freeze all backbone layers except the last 30 (the layers closest to the
classification head, counting from the output).
Train the classification head and these top 30 backbone layers at
$\text{LR}_1 = 1 \times 10^{-4}$ for up to $T_1 = 15$ epochs.
The rationale is that the high-level semantic features encoded in the top
layers are the most task-specific and require the most adaptation, whereas
the low-level texture features in the early layers are general-purpose
and should be preserved.

\textbf{Phase 2.}
Freeze the first 50 backbone layers (the earliest feature extractors from
the input).
Fine-tune all remaining layers at $\text{LR}_2 = 1 \times 10^{-5}$ for up
to $T_2 = 20$ epochs starting from the best Phase-1 checkpoint.
The reduced learning rate prevents large gradient steps from disrupting the
representation quality already achieved in Phase 1.

Both phases use:
(a) early stopping with patience $= 7$ epochs on validation loss, saving
the model at the epoch with lowest validation loss;
(b) \texttt{ReduceLROnPlateau} with patience $= 3$ (Phase 1) and
patience $= 4$ (Phase 2), halving the learning rate when validation loss
fails to improve;
(c) balanced class weights computed per run via
\texttt{sklearn.utils.class\_weight.compute\_class\_weight("balanced")} on
the training label distribution, producing weights inversely proportional to
class frequency.

The full set of hyperparameters is listed in \cref{app:hyperparams}.
\Cref{fig:transfer} summarises the two-phase protocol and shows how the
\PHNM{} pipeline uses the two checkpoints.

\subsection{Loss functions}
\label{sec:methods:loss}

\textbf{Binary cross-entropy (BCE).}
Standard sigmoid cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_\text{BCE} = -w_y \bigl[y \log\hat{p} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})\bigr]
  \label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
where $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is the true label (1 = flood) and $w_y$ is the class
weight for class $y$.
Class weights provide a global frequency correction for the
flood:non-flood $\approx 1:1.54$ imbalance in training.

\textbf{Focal loss} \citep{Lin2017}.
Focal loss addresses two problems simultaneously: class imbalance (via
the class weight $w_y$) and hard example weighting (via a modulating factor
that down-weights easy, well-classified examples within each mini-batch).
We evaluate it as an alternative to BCE to test whether local hard example
weighting provides additional benefit on top of global frequency correction.
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_\text{focal}
    = -w_y \bigl(1 - p_t\bigr)^{\gamma} \bigl[y \log\hat{p} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})\bigr]
  \label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}
where $p_t = \hat{p}$ if $y=1$ and $p_t = 1-\hat{p}$ if $y=0$, and $\gamma = 2.0$.
The factor $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ down-weights well-classified examples within each
mini-batch, concentrating gradient signal on hard cases.
We combine focal loss with balanced class weights, so class weighting provides
global frequency correction while focal loss provides local within-batch
difficulty correction.
These are distinct orthogonal interventions: a class-weighted focal loss model
up-weights the flood class globally \emph{and} down-weights easy flood examples
locally.

\subsubsection{Stage 1: Confounder analysis from the Phase-1 checkpoint}

The Phase-1 checkpoint $M_1$ is run on all \texttt{train/non\_flood/} images.
For each image, the flood probability is computed as
$\hat{p} = 1 - \sigma(\text{logit})$,
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation and the logit corresponds to the
non-flood class due to alphabetical class ordering in Keras's
\texttt{flow\_from\_directory} (class index 0 = flood, 1 = non-flood).
The per-category FP rate is computed at threshold $\tau = 0.5$, and any
category with FP rate exceeding $\theta = 0.05$ is flagged.

The central motivation for using the Phase-1 checkpoint as the mining oracle
is that training convergence destroys the mining signal.
By the end of Phase 2, the fully converged model assigns near-zero flood
probability to all training non-flood images regardless of visual similarity:
EfficientNetB0 Phase-2 achieves a training-set river FP rate of 0.31\,\%,
leaving fewer than two candidates above the 0.5 threshold across all 323
training river images.
The Phase-1 checkpoint has trained only the head and the top 30 backbone
layers; the early ImageNet feature extractors ($\sim$70\,\% of the backbone)
remain frozen.
These frozen layers produce feature representations calibrated on ImageNet
rather than on flood data, retaining genuine uncertainty on river and pool
images whose textures resemble flood water in the ImageNet feature space.
This residual uncertainty provides the difficulty signal that Stage 2 exploits.

\subsubsection{Stage 2: Difficulty-ranked candidate selection}

From the flagged categories, all images are collected and ranked by
descending Phase-1 flood probability $\hat{p}$.
The top $\pi = 10\,\%$ are selected as hard negative candidates.
Percentile-based selection is invariant to probability calibration---it
selects the images on which the Phase-1 model is most confused, not merely
those that exceeded a binary threshold.
This is important because the calibration of the Phase-1 model is not
optimised; using an absolute probability threshold would produce
inconsistent candidate set sizes across architectures and loss functions.

A partition-safety assertion is enforced before proceeding: every candidate
in $\mathcal{X}_\text{hard}$ is confirmed to be absent from the validation
set by checking file paths.
This ensures that the hard negative injection does not constitute test-set
leakage and that the resulting model's validation performance reflects
genuine generalisation.

\subsubsection{Stage 3: Augmented retraining from the Phase-2 checkpoint}

Each selected hard negative image receives $5\times$ data augmentation:
random rotation $\pm 30^{\circ}$, random zoom $\pm 30\,\%$, random
brightness scaling by a factor in $[0.6, 1.4]$, and random horizontal flip.
The augmented images are injected into the training set alongside the original
candidates.
This $5\times$ factor increases the effective weight of the hard negatives in
the training distribution without requiring loss reweighting.

The augmented training set is used to retrain from the Phase-2 checkpoint $M_2$
using the same two-phase protocol described in \cref{sec:methods:finetune}.
Starting from $M_2$ rather than from scratch ensures that the HNM model
benefits from all the representation learning performed in the original
two-phase training; the Phase-2 checkpoint is the strong converged baseline
and serves as a warm start for the confounder-targeted refinement.

categories.
Together, the three-way comparison provides a hierarchical isolation of the
\PHNM{} contribution.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:methods:eval}

\textbf{Primary metric: \PR{}.}
We motivate the use of \PR{} over ROC-AUC in \cref{sec:results:metric}.
PR-AUC is computed using \texttt{sklearn.metrics.precision\_recall\_curve}
and \texttt{sklearn.metrics.auc} on the validation set.

\textbf{Threshold-level metrics.}
At $\tau = 0.5$ we report: flood recall (TP/(TP+FN)), flood precision
(TP/(TP+FP)), flood F1, accuracy, false negative count (FN), and false
positive count (FP).
The threshold is fixed at 0.5 for all conditions; no threshold optimisation
on the validation set is performed.

\textbf{Per-category FP rates.}
For each non-flood category $c$ with $n_c$ validation images, the observed
FP count $k_c$ (non-flood images predicted as flood) is recorded and the
FP rate $r_c = k_c / n_c$ is reported with a \CP{} 95\,\% binomial CI:
\begin{equation}
  \bigl[
    B(\alpha/2;\, k_c,\, n_c - k_c + 1),\;
    B(1 - \alpha/2;\, k_c + 1,\, n_c - k_c)
  \bigr]
  \label{eq:cp}
\end{equation}
where $B(q; a, b)$ denotes the $q$-quantile of the $\text{Beta}(a,b)$
distribution and $\alpha = 0.05$.

\textbf{Statistical comparisons.}
Pairwise model comparisons use McNemar's test \citep{McNemar1947} on paired
validation predictions with Bonferroni correction for the number of pairwise
comparisons.
For per-category FP comparisons across conditions, Fisher's exact test is
used due to small cell counts.

\subsection{Baseline training dynamics}
\label{sec:results:dynamics}

\Cref{fig:training_curves} shows training and validation curves for the
\EffNet{} BCE baseline over 20 epochs (the last Phase-2 epoch; Phase-1 runs
for a maximum of 15 epochs internally).
The Phase-1 to Phase-2 boundary is visible around epoch\,7--8 as a transient
spike in training loss: when the frozen backbone layers are unfrozen and the
learning rate is reduced from $1 \times 10^{-4}$ to $1 \times 10^{-5}$, the
model briefly destabilises as previously frozen layer weights are exposed to
gradients for the first time, before re-converging.
This spike is expected under progressive fine-tuning and does not indicate
training instability.

After the spike, training loss decreases smoothly and validation loss stabilises
around 0.045--0.050.
The train-validation loss gap of approximately 0.02 at convergence indicates
mild overfitting, consistent with a 3,280-image training set.
The validation AUC (ROC) reaches 0.9980 at the best epoch and stays above
0.9960 for all final epochs, indicating stable generalisation.
The Keras \texttt{Recall} metric tracked during training measures non-flood
recall (class index 1, alphabetically first in binary classification), not
flood recall; flood recall is computed from the confusion matrix in
post-training evaluation.

\ResNet{} exhibits different dynamics: it achieves a stable Phase-2 plateau
earlier and terminates via early stopping after fewer total epochs.
Its validation AUC reaches 0.9986 at the best epoch---slightly above
\EffNet{}---yet its per-threshold performance (\eg{} flood recall at $\tau=0.5$)
is substantially worse, illustrating the disconnect between ROC-AUC and
practical operating performance.

\subsection{Baseline performance at $\tau = 0.5$}
\label{sec:results:baseline}

\Cref{tab:baseline} reports validation set performance for all four baseline
conditions at the fixed decision threshold $\tau = 0.5$.
We discuss flood precision (TP/(TP+FP)) and flood recall (TP/(TP+FN)) over
322 flood validation images and 497 non-flood validation images.

\textbf{\EffNet{} BCE.}
\EffNet{} BCE achieves 97.8\,\% flood recall with only 7 false negatives and
7 false positives.
The nearly symmetric error pattern (7\,FN, 7\,FP) indicates that the model
has identified a well-calibrated decision boundary near the ambiguous boundary
between flood and near-flood-like non-flood categories.
\PR{} of 0.9976 confirms that precision remains high across the entire
recall range.

\textbf{\EffNet{} Focal.}
Focal loss ($\gamma = 2.0$) modestly improves \EffNet{}:
flood recall increases from 97.8\,\% to 98.5\,\% (7\,FN to 5\,FN) and FP
decreases from 7 to 5.
\PR{} improves marginally from 0.9976 to 0.9977.
The consistent improvement across both FN and FP suggests that focal loss
helps \EffNet{} find a slightly better decision boundary, consistent with
the compound-scaled architecture's capacity to benefit from gradient
reweighting.

\textbf{\ResNet{} BCE.}
\ResNet{} BCE produces a highly asymmetric error pattern: 63\,FN and 17\,FP.
The 19.6\,\% flood miss rate (63/322) is operationally unacceptable for a
first-pass screening system.
Despite this, \ResNet{} achieves \emph{higher} overall accuracy (98.5\,\%
vs.\ \EffNet{}'s 98.3\,\%), illustrating the accuracy paradox discussed in
\cref{sec:results:metric}: the larger non-flood class (497 images) dominates
the accuracy denominator, masking the systematic flood miss rate.
\PR{} of 0.9614 correctly captures this failure.

\textbf{\ResNet{} Focal.}
Focal loss substantially \emph{degrades} \ResNet{}: recall drops from 80.4\,\%
to 71.1\,\% (63\,FN to 93\,FN), while FP decreases from 17 to 10.
The net effect is a model with worse flood recall but better non-flood
precision---the opposite of the desired recall-first objective.
\PR{} decreases from 0.9614 to 0.9600.
This divergence between focal loss effects on the two architectures
is discussed further in \cref{sec:discussion:loss}.

\subsection{PR-AUC versus ROC-AUC: the metric matters}
\label{sec:results:metric}

\Cref{fig:prroc} illustrates the metric discrepancy directly.
The ROC curves of both baseline BCE models appear respectable: \ResNet{}'s
ROC-AUC of 0.9728 is only 2.6 points below \EffNet{}'s 0.9985.
A practitioner relying solely on ROC-AUC would conclude that \ResNet{} is
``nearly as good'' as \EffNet{}.

The precision-recall curves reveal the true picture.
\ResNet{}'s precision collapses rapidly as recall exceeds approximately 0.85:
to achieve 95\,\% flood recall, a practitioner would need to accept a threshold
at which precision drops substantially below the no-skill baseline.
The PR-AUC gap---0.9976 vs.\ 0.9614, a difference of 3.6 points---directly
quantifies this precision loss across the full recall range and accurately
reflects the difference of 56 missed floods (63 FN vs.\ 7 FN).

The accuracy paradox compounds this picture.
\ResNet{} achieves 98.5\,\% accuracy vs.\ \EffNet{}'s 98.3\,\%---a difference
of 0.2 points in the direction that would mislead a practitioner into
preferring \ResNet{}.
The mechanism is that \ResNet{}'s 63 missed floods reduce the accuracy score
by $63/819 = 7.7\,\%$ (relative to the non-flood majority), but this is more
than compensated by \ResNet{}'s lower FP rate (17 vs.\ 7), which correctly
classifies 10 additional non-flood images.
PR-AUC correctly identifies \EffNet{} as the superior model for this
recall-first screening problem.
This empirically confirms the theoretical argument of \citet{Davis2006} and
the practical guidance of \citet{Saito2015}.

the images show reflective water surfaces, ripple patterns, and partially
submerged vegetation or riverbank infrastructure that are visually
indistinguishable from flooded road surfaces without wider field-of-view
context.
The 7 false negatives are flood scenes with attenuated water signal: dimly
lit scenes, partially visible flooding in the background, or scenes where
the dominant visual element is a dry foreground with flooding visible only
in a portion of the frame.
These are genuine boundary cases; a human reviewer presented with the same
images would face similar uncertainty.

\ResNet{}'s error pattern is qualitatively different.
While its false positives are also river-scene confounders, its 63 false
negatives include many prototypical, unambiguous flood scenes---well-lit road
surfaces covered with water, visible debris, and clear flooding indicators
that a human would immediately classify as flood.
This pattern is characteristic of systematic decision-boundary collapse
rather than ambiguity-driven misclassification: \ResNet{}'s boundary is
miscalibrated, not merely challenged by hard cases.
This distinction has important implications for remediation: the Phase-1 HNM
approach is designed to address confounder-driven FP errors (relevant to
\EffNet{}) rather than systematic FN bias (the primary failure mode of
\ResNet{}).

\subsection{Confounder analysis}
\label{sec:results:confounder}

\Cref{tab:confounder} reports per-category false positive rates on the
validation set for both BCE baselines.
This table is the primary deliverable of the confounder analysis: it shows
which non-flood visual categories drive false positives at a statistically
characterised level.

River images are the dominant confounder for both architectures.
\EffNet{} BCE produces 7 river FPs in 76 validation river images
(9.2\,\%, 95\,\% CI: [3.8\,\%, 17.7\,\%]).
\ResNet{} BCE produces 3 river FPs (3.9\,\%, CI: [0.8\,\%, 11.0\,\%]).
The plant category produces 1 FP for \EffNet{} (2.9\,\%), zero for \ResNet{}.
All other non-flood categories---animal, building, park walkway,
street clear, swimming pool, vehicle---produce zero false positives at
$\tau = 0.5$ for both models.

The interplay between the \EffNet{} and \ResNet{} results is instructive.
\EffNet{} has a higher river FP rate (9.2\,\% vs.\ 3.9\,\%) but dramatically
better flood recall (97.8\,\% vs.\ 80.4\,\%).
The mechanism is a trade-off between flood sensitivity and non-flood
specificity on the river category: \EffNet{}'s more sensitive decision
boundary catches more floods but also catches more rivers, whereas
\ResNet{}'s less sensitive boundary misses many floods while avoiding
river FPs.
For a recall-first screening system, the \EffNet{} operating point is
strongly preferable: accepting 9.2\,\% river FP rate in exchange for
97.8\,\% flood recall versus 80.4\,\% flood recall is an advantageous trade.

The wide \CP{} CIs for categories with zero observed FP deserve emphasis.
For swimming pool ($N = 28$), the 95\,\% upper CI bound is 12.3\,\%---meaning
that if a future deployment generated 28 new swimming pool images, we would
expect with 95\,\% probability to see at least 0 and at most 3--4 FPs.
A ``zero FP on swimming pool'' result does not imply robustness; it implies
insufficient statistical power.

\subsection{Phase-1 HNM ablation results}
\label{sec:results:hnm}

\Cref{tab:hnm} reports ablation results, \cref{fig:fp_bar} shows the
river false positive rates across all conditions with \CP{} confidence
intervals, and \cref{fig:hnm_ablation} compares PR and ROC curves for
the four \EffNet{} ablation conditions.

\textbf{Overall accuracy ordering.}
For \EffNet{} BCE, the strict ordering HNM ($98.78\,\%$) $>$ extended
training ($98.66\,\%$) $>$ random injection ($98.41\,\%$) $>$ baseline
($98.29\,\%$) confirms the hierarchical isolation:
(1) any additional training beyond Phase 2 improves performance over
    the baseline;
(2) injecting category-matched data improves performance over extended
    training alone (random injection vs.\ extended training);
(3) difficulty-ranked injection provides the largest gain (HNM vs.\
    random injection).
The 0.49 accuracy percentage point improvement from HNM over baseline is the
largest absolute accuracy gain across conditions (single seed; no significance
test on overall accuracy has been conducted).
The strict ordering is consistent with difficulty-ranked selection contributing
beyond additional training epochs and category exposure alone.

For \ResNet{} BCE, HNM improves accuracy by 0.13\,pp (98.66\,\% vs.\
98.53\,\%) over the baseline.
The smaller gain is consistent with \ResNet{}'s fundamentally different
failure mode: its 63 missed floods (FN bias) are not addressed by
confounder-targeted HNM, which is designed to reduce FP rate on the
non-flood side of the boundary.

\textbf{River FP rates.}
\PHNM{} produced no statistically significant change in river FP rate for
either architecture (McNemar $p > 0.05$ after Bonferroni correction for all
pairwise comparisons; \CP{} CIs overlap substantially across all conditions).
For \EffNet{} BCE, \PHNM{} and baseline both produce 7/76 river FPs (9.2\,\%).
Extended training reduces this to 5/76 (6.6\,\%), and random injection
returns to 7/76 (9.2\,\%), but none of these differences are detectable at
$N_\text{river} = 76$.
For \ResNet{} BCE, \PHNM{} produces 4/76 (5.3\,\%) versus baseline 3/76
(3.9\,\%); this single-image change is within noise.
Power analysis indicates that a sample size of approximately 300 river
validation images would be needed to detect a 3--4 percentage point FP rate
reduction at 80\,\% power and $\alpha = 0.05$.

\textbf{\ResNet{} Focal HNM: decision-boundary collapse.}
The \ResNet{} Focal HNM condition produced results consistent with the
baseline focal loss collapse: validation accuracy $\approx 87.4\,\%$,
flood recall $\approx 71\,\%$, river FP rate $= 3.9\,\%$ (same as baseline).
The HNM pipeline cannot recover a model whose decision boundary has
collapsed under focal loss training.
This finding reinforces an important conclusion: gradient-level reweighting
(focal loss) and data-level augmentation (HNM) are not equivalent
interventions and are not interchangeable.
For \ResNet{}, only BCE provides a viable training path; HNM from a Focal
baseline provides no benefit.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why PR-AUC reveals what accuracy hides}
\label{sec:discussion:metric}

The accuracy paradox in \cref{tab:baseline} provides a concrete illustration
of the metric selection problem.
\ResNet{} achieves \emph{higher} overall accuracy (98.5\,\%) than \EffNet{}
(98.3\,\%) yet misses nine times as many floods (63 vs.\ 7) and has \PR{} that
is 3.6 points lower.
The mathematical mechanism is straightforward: the 819-image validation set
contains 497 non-flood images (60.7\,\%) and 322 flood images (39.3\,\%).
\ResNet{}'s systematic non-flood bias means it correctly classifies most
of the 497 non-flood images (480/497) while misclassifying 63 of the 322
flood images.
The accuracy calculation weights each correct classification equally,
so the large non-flood majority dominates: $\frac{259 + 480}{819} = 90.2\,\%$
base accuracy without correctly classifying any floods would be 60.7\,\%.
The 98.5\,\% figure reflects mostly non-flood accuracy with little flood
accuracy contribution.

ROC-AUC reveals a meaningful difference (0.9985 vs.\ 0.9728) but quantifies
it less sharply than PR-AUC because true negative rate (specificity) enters
the denominator of the ROC calculation.
With 480 true negatives out of 497 (96.6\,\%), the specificity is very high
for both models, inflating ROC-AUC.
PR-AUC replaces specificity with precision, directly penalising false positives
that reduce precision; and it penalises false negatives through the recall axis.
This double penalisation of both error types in the direction that matters for
screening makes \PR{} the appropriate primary metric.

We therefore recommend that all flood screening evaluations report \PR{} as
the primary metric alongside per-category FP rates with confidence intervals.
Overall accuracy should be treated as a secondary metric at best; ROC-AUC
as a supplementary one.

\subsection{Architecture matters more than loss function for ResNet50}
\label{sec:discussion:loss}

The divergent response to focal loss between \EffNet{} and \ResNet{} is
one of the more striking empirical findings of this paper.
Both architectures were trained with balanced class weights, which provide
global frequency correction for the flood:non-flood imbalance.
Despite this, \ResNet{} BCE achieves only 80.4\,\% flood recall, and focal
loss ($\gamma=2.0$) makes it substantially worse (71.1\,\%, 93\,FN).
\EffNet{} responds positively to both interventions: focal loss reduces FN
from 7 to 5 and FP from 7 to 5.

The explanation likely lies in the quality of the learned flood representation.
\EffNet{}'s compound-scaled architecture \citep{Tan2019} balances depth,
width, and resolution, producing richer feature representations from
ImageNet transfer that encode fine-grained texture distinctions relevant to
flood detection.
Near the decision boundary, \EffNet{}'s representations are diverse enough
that gradient reweighting (focal loss) can find a better boundary by
focusing on the most informative hard cases.
\ResNet{}, with its larger parameter count (25.6\,M vs.\ 5.3\,M), appears to
overfit the dominant non-flood pattern despite balanced class weighting,
producing a decision boundary that systematically assigns low flood probability.
In this regime, focal loss amplifies the problem: by down-weighting the
well-classified easy non-flood examples, it further reduces gradient signal
from the misclassified flood examples, accelerating the collapse.

The practical implication is important: focal loss is not a universally
beneficial intervention for flood screening, and its effect depends on
whether the architecture's feature space has learned a quality flood
representation.
Class weighting ($\neq$ focal loss) is the safer default; focal loss should
be applied only after verifying that the model's flood recall is already
near-plateau.

\subsection{Why Phase-1 mining works: convergence, uncertainty, and the offline HNM constraint}
\label{sec:discussion:phase1}

As described in \cref{sec:methods:hnm}, training convergence eliminates the
mining signal; the Phase-1 checkpoint retains genuine uncertainty on visual
confounders that the Phase-2 checkpoint has resolved.

Phase-1 mining solves this by identifying an intermediate checkpoint at
which the model retains meaningful uncertainty on hard negatives.
This is analogous to knowledge distillation \citep{Guo2017}: intermediate
representations in partially trained models often encode more generalisable
information than the final model, which has ``compressed'' its uncertainty
into confident predictions.
By using the Phase-1 model's uncertainty as a proxy for the visual
ambiguity between flood and confounder categories, we recover a useful
signal that the Phase-2 model has suppressed.

The improvement in overall classification accuracy (0.49 accuracy percentage points for \EffNet{})
provides empirical evidence that Phase-1 mining identifies images that are
genuinely informative for refining the decision boundary, rather than
simply adding more training data.
The strict ordering HNM $>$ extended training $>$ random injection isolates
this contribution to the difficulty ranking itself.

\subsection{Interpreting the HNM river FP results}
\label{sec:discussion:fp}

The null result for river FP rate reduction under \PHNM{} merits careful
interpretation.
The HNM pipeline produces 7/76 river FPs both before and after mining
for \EffNet{}---identical to baseline---while overall accuracy improves
by 0.49 accuracy percentage points.
Several explanations are consistent with this pattern.

First, the improvements in overall accuracy may arise from better
classification of flood images at the decision boundary (reduced FN)
rather than from better rejection of river images (reduced FP).
The additional hard negatives from the HNM pipeline correct the model's
boundary in the flood-adjacent region, improving flood recall, while
the river-adjacent region of the boundary---corresponding to the most
visually ambiguous river images---remains difficult regardless of training
condition.

Second, the validation sample size is insufficient to detect the difference.
With $N_\text{river} = 76$ and an observed FP rate near 9\,\%, a reduction
of 2 FPs (from 7 to 5) would be needed to reach the extended-training level
of 6.6\,\%; this requires a sample of approximately 300 river images to
be detectable at standard power levels.
The null result is consistent with no true effect, with a true effect below the detectable threshold at this sample size, or with genuine visual indistinguishability of the hardest river images from floods.

Third, the most visually confusing river images---the 7 FPs in both baseline
and HNM conditions---may be genuinely indistinguishable from floods without
additional context, in which case no training intervention short of providing
additional contextual cues would reduce the error.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single seed.}
        All results are from seed\,=\,42; multi-seed replication (seeds
        1, 7, 123, 2024) is in progress.
        Bootstrap CIs and McNemar tests cannot be reported until seed
        aggregation is complete.
        This limits the statistical strength of the ablation conclusions.

  \item \textbf{Small non-flood $N$ for water categories.}
        Swimming pool ($N_\text{val}{=}28$) and plant ($N_\text{val}{=}34$)
        have validation sets too small to draw conclusions about confounder
        robustness.
        The very categories most operationally relevant for false-positive
        reduction (pools are common near flooded residential areas) are
        the categories where we have the least statistical power.

  \item \textbf{Single geographic and source distribution.}
        The dataset sources have not been fully characterised for temporal
        or geographic clustering; USF FloodingDataset images are primarily
        from North American flood events.
        The RIWA river images come from diverse European and North American
        river systems.
        Generalisation to tropical, Asian, or African flood environments
        is not validated.

  \item \textbf{Phase boundary not ablated.}
        The (30, 50) freeze boundary is a fixed design choice motivated by
        prior progressive fine-tuning literature; optimal values for
        flood-specific data are unknown.
        Different boundary choices might produce stronger or weaker
        Phase-1 mining signals.

  \item \textbf{No external test set.}
        All evaluation is on the held-out validation split.
        Out-of-distribution generalisation to images from different
        cameras, weather conditions, flood types, or geographic locations
        is not assessed.
        External validation should be a priority for any deployment.

  \item \textbf{Phase-2 mining produces zero candidates.}
        By the end of Phase~2, the fully converged model assigns near-zero
        flood probability to all training non-flood images.
        Phase-2 mining was therefore not pursued; this structural property
        of converged offline models is the direct motivation for Phase-1 mining.
        Offline HNM from fully converged checkpoints is a known limitation
        reported in other applications \citep{Viola2004}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Practitioner recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion:recs}

Based on the evidence in this paper, we offer the following practical
recommendations for teams building street-level flood screening systems:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Report PR-AUC} as the primary evaluation metric, not
        accuracy or ROC-AUC.
        Report per-category FP rates with \CP{} confidence intervals for
        any category with $N_\text{val} < 100$.

  \item \textbf{Prefer \EffNet{}} over \ResNet{} for binary flood screening.
        EfficientNet's compound-scaled features provide substantially better
        flood recall (97.8\,\% vs.\ 80.4\,\%), the primary operational
        requirement for a screening system.

  \item \textbf{Apply \PHNM{}} when the Phase-1 river or pool FP rate
        exceeds 5\,\%.
        Mine from the Phase-1 checkpoint, not the converged Phase-2 model.
        Expect improvement in overall accuracy; expect that FP rate
        reduction will require larger validation sets to confirm statistically.

  \item \textbf{Do not apply focal loss} to models that already exhibit
        systematic flood under-detection.
        Verify that flood recall is near-plateau before applying focal loss;
        use BCE + balanced class weights as the baseline.

  \item \textbf{Expand water-confounder validation sets} before deployment,
        particularly for swimming pools and lakes which may co-occur
        spatially with floods in residential areas.
\end{enumerate}

%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We presented Phase-1 Hard Negative Mining (\PHNM{}), a three-stage pipeline
for improving street-level flood detection by exploiting an intermediate
training checkpoint as a mining oracle.
The core insight is that training convergence destroys the mining signal
needed for productive hard negative selection: a fully converged Phase-2
model assigns near-zero flood probability to all training non-flood images,
leaving no candidates.
The Phase-1 checkpoint, which has trained only the classification head and
top backbone layers, retains genuine uncertainty on visually ambiguous
categories and provides a meaningful difficulty ranking.

On a 4,099-image benchmark spanning 11 fine-grained categories, we
demonstrated that \EffNet{} achieves PR-AUC\,=\,0.9976 versus \ResNet{}'s
0.9614---a 3.6-point gap that is invisible in ROC-AUC (0.9985 vs.\ 0.9728).
This empirically confirms that PR-AUC is the appropriate primary metric
for this class-imbalanced screening problem.
The gap corresponds to a difference of 56 missed floods (63 vs.\ 7) in an
819-image validation set, a difference that aggregate accuracy (98.5\,\% vs.\
98.3\,\%) actually inverts.

Phase-1 HNM improves overall classification accuracy by 0.49 accuracy percentage points for
\EffNet{} BCE, outperforming extended training (+0.37\,pp) and random
injection (+0.12\,pp), confirming that difficulty-ranked selection
contributes beyond additional training epochs and category-exposure alone.
The per-category river FP reduction does not reach statistical significance
at $N_\text{river} = 76$; power analysis indicates that approximately
300 river validation images would be needed to detect a 3--4 percentage
point reduction.

Per-category FP analysis with \CP{} confidence intervals reveals that river
is the dominant confounder for both architectures; all other categories
produce zero false positives at $\tau = 0.5$.
Wide CIs for small-$N$ categories (swimming pool, plant) highlight that
``zero FP'' cannot be equated with confounder robustness without larger
evaluation sets.

We recommend that flood screening systems be evaluated at the per-category
FP rate level with explicit confidence intervals, that PR-AUC replace accuracy
and ROC-AUC as the primary reported metric, and that Phase-1 mining be
applied when any water-confounder category produces a training-set FP rate
above 5\,\%.
Future work should expand category-specific validation samples, conduct
multi-seed replication to provide bootstrap confidence intervals, and evaluate
out-of-distribution generalisation on geographically diverse datasets.
